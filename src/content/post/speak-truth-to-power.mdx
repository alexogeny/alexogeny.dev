The briefing room appears when we need it. Sometimes it's a reclaimed library, sometimes it's the server closet that smells
like ozone. Power walks in wearing a badge that says VP, but that's just a costume. In this narrative, power is anyone who can
freeze a roadmap with a glance.

We built a ritual to disarm that freeze. Before the meeting, the team sends out a "temperature packet" — a zine of annotated
charts, transcripts, and audio clips. The packet never assigns blame; it invites questions. By the time the briefing starts,
truth is already in the room, humming gently between the chairs.

## Establish the stage directions

We name a narrator for each session, and they change every time. Their job is to keep the pace humane, not to defend the work.
There are scripted pauses where anyone can call for a sidebar. If the conversation turns sharp, the narrator pulls a "scene
change" card and everyone takes three minutes to write what they actually meant to say. It's dramatic, but it keeps us from
performing courage while secretly shutting down.

## Protect the archives

These stories travel. We store them as oral histories with transcripts, never as legal documents. When someone new joins the
team, they get a welcome packet with our favorite past briefings — not for gossip, but to learn how honesty sounds here. No one
is obligated to read them, yet everyone does. The archives feel like folklore, and folklore is how we remember who we're trying
to be.

## Share the microphone

Power listens because it has to. We've built a culture where the mic rotates, and the person with the least institutional
authority opens and closes the session. It's a way to remind us that truth often arrives from the edges. The executives pretend
this is their idea. We let them. The ritual works either way.

Maybe none of this is happening in your timeline. That's okay. Consider this a script left on the stage after the actors struck
the set. Pick it up when you're ready for a different ending.
