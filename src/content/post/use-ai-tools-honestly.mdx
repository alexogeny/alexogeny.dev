Our studio exists in a liminal office that hums with analog synths and server fans. There are no NDAs on the wall, just a
chalkboard that reads "If a machine helped, say so." Clients find it disarming. That's deliberate. We grew tired of the wink
and nod that usually accompanies AI usage, so we wrote our own etiquette.

## Announce the ensemble

Every deliverable ships with liner notes: which models played which parts, which datasets set the tempo, which human edited the
final mix. The liner notes are sometimes longer than the work itself. No one has complained. In fact, clients started asking
for the notes first because they're a map of how decisions were made. Transparency turned into a collaboration prompt.

## Keep the loop audible

We run "call and response" sessions before any model goes into production. Someone feeds the prompt, someone else narrates what
the tool returns, and a third person names the risks out loud. It's messy and occasionally funny — like rehearsing improv with
a polite robot. But the ritual means no one hides behind automation. We know exactly which parts are machine artifacts and
which were coaxed by hand.

## Archive the glitches

Honesty isn't just about credit; it's about memory. Every odd output, hallucinated citation, or quietly biased suggestion gets
catalogued in our "glitch atlas." Future us can scan the atlas before a new engagement and remember where the models stumble.
We even invite clients to add their own sightings. It's not a shame wall. It's a travelogue of the weird paths we've taken
together.

The studio is fictional, but the practice doesn't have to be. If you're going to share a stage with silicon bandmates, give
them proper billing and keep the lights bright enough that everyone can see how the song was made.
